\begin{abstract}
Online multi-object tracking relies on matched detections to continually update track states. However, a detection that supports the current association may still cause a harmful update. An inaccurate bounding box or a corrupted appearance feature can affect the track state and influence later predictions and associations. The same detection may also benefit one state while harming another, making association acceptance insufficient to determine how it should update a track. To address this problem, we propose SHUTrack, a framework that learns to suppress harmful state updates after association. Its central idea is to assess a matched detection by the consequences of using it to update the existing track state. During training, SHUTrack compares the subsequent effects of updating the state and retaining the state before that update, providing a learning signal for identifying potentially harmful updates. Guided by this signal, it controls motion and appearance updates separately using the available track context. This allows the tracker to limit harmful updates while preserving its ability to adapt to target changes. Experiments on MOT17, MOT20, and SportsMOT demonstrate consistent improvements over TrackTrack, with HOTA scores of 67.8, 66.4, and 76.5, respectively. These results support learned state-update control as an effective way to maintain reliable trajectories across different tracking conditions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Multi-object tracking (MOT) aims to localize multiple objects in video and maintain their identities over time. It provides the temporal information needed for autonomous driving \cite{geiger2012we}, intelligent surveillance \cite{yin2019large}, sports analytics \cite{cui2023sportsmot}, and human behavior analysis \cite{kong2022human}. Tracking-by-detection (TBD) methods first detect objects in each frame and then associate detections across frames to form trajectories \cite{bewley2016simple,wojke2017simple,zhang2022bytetrack}. As a trajectory develops, matched detections continually update the information used to track the target. Maintaining reliable trajectories therefore requires careful use of these detections, especially when occlusion, crowding, and target changes make their location or appearance uncertain.

State updating connects current detections with future tracking decisions. In many online TBD trackers, a motion state describes the target's location and movement, while an appearance state stores identity information from previous detections. These states support later predictions and associations and must be updated as the target changes. Each update contributes to the information accumulated along the trajectory and shapes how later detections are interpreted. SORT \cite{bewley2016simple} and DeepSORT \cite{wojke2017simple} established efficient tracking pipelines based on motion estimation and appearance matching. Subsequent methods improve the use of detections within these pipelines: ByteTrack \cite{zhang2022bytetrack} recovers low-confidence detections, OC-SORT \cite{cao2023observation} uses detection history to improve motion estimation, and StrongSORT \cite{du2023strongsort} and BoT-SORT \cite{aharon2022bot} strengthen appearance and motion handling. TrackTrack \cite{shim2025focusing} further considers detection candidates from the perspective of existing tracks. These advances help trackers obtain useful information from available detections. However, a detection selected for association can still contain information that damages the state it updates. Once that information is retained, its influence can continue through later frames, even if the current detection was assigned to the correct target.

Existing methods reduce unreliable updates through detection confidence and historical context. ConfTrack \cite{jung2024conftrack} adjusts filtering according to detection confidence, while Deep OC-SORT \cite{maggiolino2023deep} uses confidence to adapt appearance updates. MeMOT \cite{cai2022memot} and MeMOTR \cite{gao2023memotr} use learned memory to make better use of historical information. These approaches help trackers handle differences in detection quality and temporal evidence. Yet the quality of a detection does not directly determine the consequence of updating a particular track state. A high-confidence detection may damage a well-maintained state, whereas a low-confidence detection may help correct a state that has drifted. Historical consistency is also ambiguous when a real change in target motion or appearance conflicts with earlier detections. This motivates learning to assess a matched detection through its update consequences: whether using it improves the subsequent state compared with retaining the state before that update.

Fig.~\ref{fig:motivation} illustrates the distinction between accepting a detection and controlling its state updates. The same matched detection can require different update strengths for motion and appearance. For example, under partial occlusion, an accurate bounding box may support motion estimation while the appearance feature contains information from another person. Reducing the appearance update can protect the appearance state while preserving the useful motion update. Conversely, a shifted bounding box may provide an unreliable motion update even when its appearance feature remains useful. A harmful update can bias the evidence used for the next association and make a short detection error affect a longer part of the trajectory. Applying the same response to both states can either retain harmful information or discard useful information. These cases motivate separate update decisions for the two states, with suppression guided by the effect of each update on the existing track.

To address this problem, we propose SHUTrack, which learns to suppress harmful state updates after association. During training, it compares the subsequent effects of updating a track state with a matched detection and retaining the state before that update. This provides a learning signal for assessing whether the detection should influence the state. The Harm-Aware Update Controller (HUC) uses recent track history to make separate update decisions for motion and appearance. The Reliability-Guided Update Calibrator (RUC) then refines these decisions according to the reliability of the available evidence. Together, they reduce the influence of detections that may harm the corresponding state while allowing useful updates to follow target changes. SHUTrack retains the current match and applies its update decisions before the next tracking step. Online decisions use only current and past information.

Through this design, SHUTrack makes the effect of a matched detection on future track states an explicit part of online tracking. We evaluate the framework on MOT17 \cite{dendorfer2021motchallenge}, MOT20 \cite{dendorfer2020mot20}, and SportsMOT \cite{cui2023sportsmot}, covering pedestrian scenes, dense crowds, and fast team-sport motion. Experiments with TrackTrack as the host demonstrate consistent improvements in tracking performance and identity continuity across the three benchmarks. Component ablations further examine the roles of motion and appearance update control and reliability-guided refinement. These results support harmful-update suppression as a useful approach to maintaining reliable trajectories.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate harmful state updates as a distinct challenge in online multi-object tracking, highlighting how errors in accepted detections can persist through track states and affect subsequent tracking decisions.

    \item We propose learning to assess matched detections by their update consequences. This perspective accounts for the different effects that the same detection can have on track states and provides a basis for selectively suppressing harmful updates.

    \item We present a tracking framework that balances the preservation of reliable track states with adaptation to target changes. Experiments on MOT17, MOT20, and SportsMOT demonstrate its effectiveness in maintaining identity continuity across different tracking conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Online Multi-Object Tracking}
Tracking-by-detection is widely used in online MOT because it separates object detection from temporal association and supports efficient tracking pipelines \cite{ciaparrone2020deep}. SORT \cite{bewley2016simple} combines Kalman prediction with Hungarian assignment, while DeepSORT \cite{wojke2017simple} introduces appearance features to improve identity matching. BoT-SORT and StrongSORT strengthen this framework through improved motion handling and appearance modeling \cite{aharon2022bot,du2023strongsort}. ByteTrack \cite{zhang2022bytetrack} recovers unmatched tracks using low-confidence detections, showing that detections with uncertain scores can still support trajectory continuity. More recent methods improve the evidence used for association. SparseTrack \cite{liu2025sparsetrack} decomposes crowded scenes using pseudo-depth, and FineTrack \cite{ren2023focus} learns fine-grained appearance representations. AIPT \cite{zhang2024aipt} and RccTrack \cite{zhang2025robust} improve motion and appearance handling under camera movement and target ambiguity. TrackTrack \cite{shim2025focusing} further organizes high-confidence, low-confidence, and redundant detection candidates from the perspective of existing tracks.

Temporal modeling provides another way to improve tracking. MotionTrack \cite{xiao2024motiontrack} learns trajectory prediction from historical motion, DiffMOT \cite{lv2024diffmot} models nonlinear motion through diffusion, and MambaTrack and TrackSSM use state-space models to capture temporal dependencies \cite{xiao2024mambatrack,hu2026trackssm}. Query-based methods such as TrackFormer \cite{meinhardt2022trackformer} and MOTR \cite{zeng2022motr} integrate detection and association through representations propagated across frames. These approaches improve how a tracker predicts targets or links them over time. In modular TBD trackers, matched detections also update the states used in subsequent frames, creating a further opportunity to improve tracking beyond the current assignment. SHUTrack focuses on this stage: it learns how strongly a matched detection should update the motion and appearance states according to the potential consequences of that update.

\subsection{Track State Updating}
Motion state updates combine a track prediction with a newly matched detection. The Kalman filter \cite{kalman1960new} controls their relative influence through uncertainty estimates. BoT-SORT \cite{aharon2022bot} improves motion handling with camera-motion compensation, while OC-SORT \cite{cao2023observation} uses detection history to correct accumulated prediction errors after occlusion. Other methods directly adjust the influence of detections during filtering. The adaptive Kalman filter in GIAOTracker \cite{du2021giaotracker} scales measurement uncertainty according to detection confidence, and ConfTrack \cite{jung2024conftrack} also uses confidence to limit unreliable corrections. Deep LG-Track \cite{meng2025deep} considers both detection confidence and trajectory disappearance when adjusting the filter. These methods show that the strength of a motion update should depend on the reliability of the detection and the existing prediction. However, these confidence-based rules do not directly compare the later effects of applying and skipping the current update.

Appearance state updates face a similar need to balance adaptation with the preservation of identity information. DeepSORT \cite{wojke2017simple} maintains a gallery of appearance features, whereas StrongSORT \cite{du2023strongsort} uses an exponential moving average to maintain a compact appearance state. Deep OC-SORT \cite{maggiolino2023deep} adapts the update weight using detection confidence. Deep LG-Track \cite{meng2025deep} further accounts for localization quality and appearance clarity when combining historical and current features. Learned memory methods, including multi-track pooling \cite{kim2021discriminative}, MeMOT \cite{cai2022memot}, and MeMOTR \cite{gao2023memotr}, use temporal information to maintain more informative identity representations. These approaches reduce dependence on a single frame, but the reliability of a current feature remains important because its influence can persist in the stored appearance state.

Learning update behavior has also been studied in single-object tracking. UpdateNet \cite{zhang2019learning} learns a template update function from initial, historical, and current templates. Meta-Updater \cite{dai2020high} uses temporal geometric, discriminative, and appearance cues to decide whether to update the tracker. Its training labels are based on current localization quality. These studies provide important precedents for learned updates, while the confidence-based MOT methods above already reduce the influence of unreliable detections. SHUTrack builds on this line of work by learning from the subsequent effects of alternative update choices. It compares updating a state with retaining that state before the update, providing separate supervision for motion and appearance. A detection can consequently receive different update strengths for the two states, with suppression guided by its estimated effect on subsequent state quality.

\subsection{Counterfactual Supervision}
Counterfactual reasoning considers how an outcome would change under an alternative action. In sequential decision making, counterfactually guided policy search uses structural causal models and recorded experience to evaluate alternative policies \cite{buesing2018woulda}. This offers a relevant principle for update control: the value of an action can be assessed by comparing its consequences with those of another choice. A separate connection is learning using privileged information \cite{vapnik2009new}, where information available during training helps a model that operates without that information at deployment. These ideas concern different aspects of supervision: comparing alternative outcomes and learning from additional training information.

SHUTrack applies these ideas to a local state-update decision. During offline training, updating and retaining the state are evaluated from the same starting state using shared future evidence. Their subsequent state errors provide supervision for learning when an update may be harmful. The comparison is confined to the selected state update under fixed future evidence; it does not estimate the outcome of rerunning the full tracker with different associations. Future annotations are used to construct training targets, while online update control uses only current and past track information. This connects the supervision to the consequences of updating a particular state and preserves the online tracking setting.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Framework Overview}
SHUTrack learns to control how matched detections update the states used for later tracking. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, the tracker first predicts track locations and associates detections with existing tracks. For each matched track with enough history, SHUTrack forms an event from the current detection, the state before its update, and the matching information. The recent event history helps determine how strongly the detection should influence the motion and appearance states.

The Harm-Aware Update Controller (HUC) produces two initial update weights from this history. The Reliability-Guided Update Calibrator (RUC) refines them by relating the separate evidence histories to the reliability of the current decision. The resulting weights control the two state updates independently. During training, supervision is obtained by comparing the consequences of applying an update and retaining the state before that update. Online inference requires only current and past information.

\begin{figure}[pos=!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig2_framework_v12_cec.png}
    \caption{Overview of SHUTrack. After association, the matched detection and the track state before updating form an event in the recent track history. HUC predicts separate motion and appearance update weights, and RUC refines them using history and reliability information. The final weights control the state updates used for later tracking. Supervision from update consequences is constructed offline.}
    \label{fig:framework}
\end{figure}

Update control is applied to tracks in the tracked or lost state with at least $T$ matched detections in their history, where $T$ is the input context length. Each input window contains the current event and up to $T-1$ past events, with shorter histories left-padded and masked. Unmatched events retain track information and indicate that no detection is available. This distinguishes a continuous sequence of matches from a return after a gap.

\subsection{State Update Control and Supervision}
\paragraph{Motion and appearance updates.}
For clarity, we omit the track index. Before updating at frame $t$, the track has a predicted motion state $(\bm{\mu}_t^{-},\bm{P}_t^{-})$ and an appearance state $\bm{f}_t^{-}$. Its matched detection is $d_t=(\bm{z}_t,\bm{q}_t,s_t)$, where $\bm{z}_t$ is the bounding-box measurement, $\bm{q}_t$ is the appearance feature, and $s_t$ is the detection confidence. SHUTrack predicts $\gfinal=[\gm,\ga]\in[0,1]^2$.

The Kalman filter first computes its full update:
\begin{equation}
(\bm{\mu}_t^{+},\bm{P}_t^{+})
=\operatorname{KFUpdate}(\bm{\mu}_t^{-},\bm{P}_t^{-},\bm{z}_t,s_t).
\label{eq:full_kf}
\end{equation}
The motion weight then controls the change from the prediction to this updated state:
\begin{align}
\bm{\mu}_t &= \bm{\mu}_t^{-}+\gm(\bm{\mu}_t^{+}-\bm{\mu}_t^{-}),\\
\bm{P}_t &= (1-\gm)\bm{P}_t^{-}+\gm\bm{P}_t^{+}.
\label{eq:motion_assimilation}
\end{align}
The covariance is symmetrized after interpolation. The box stored in track history is interpolated between the predicted box and the detection box using the same weight, and the velocity cues are updated from this effective box. Thus, motion update control also affects the geometric history used in later associations.

For appearance, let the unnormalized candidate produced by an exponential moving average (EMA) be
\begin{align}
\beta_t &= \alpha+(1-\alpha)(1-s_t),\\
\bar{\bm{f}}_t^{+} &= \beta_t\bm{f}_t^{-}+(1-\beta_t)\bm{q}_t,
\label{eq:full_ema}
\end{align}
where $\alpha$ is the appearance decay factor. The controlled update is
\begin{equation}
\bm{f}_t=\operatorname{norm}\!\left(
(1-\ga)\bm{f}_t^{-}+\ga\bar{\bm{f}}_t^{+}
\right).
\label{eq:appearance_assimilation}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{norm}$ means $\ell_2$ normalization, applied after the weighted combination. In implementation, a zero weight directly retains the stored feature, and a weight of one applies the full EMA update. For both states, zero preserves the state before the current update, one applies the full update, and intermediate values reduce its influence. Separate weights allow a matched detection to contribute differently to motion and appearance.

\paragraph{Comparing update consequences.}
Tracking annotations specify target locations and identities, but do not directly label whether a particular state update is beneficial. We construct this supervision offline using paired rollouts. For each state type $r\in\{m,a\}$, two branches start from the same state $S_{t,r}^{-}$. The update branch, denoted $W$, applies the full current update; the hold branch, denoted $H$, retains $S_{t,r}^{-}$. Both then follow the same update rules and receive the same future detection evidence over the next $K$ frames.

The target identity is determined from matches to ground truth before the current event. Future detections are selected by their overlap with this target's ground-truth box, subject to $\theta_{\mathrm{match}}$. Ground truth guides detection selection and loss evaluation, while the state updates use detector outputs. This comparison measures the local effect of the current update under shared future evidence; it does not rerun global association.

\paragraph{Motion supervision.}
At each future step, both branches undergo the same camera compensation and prediction operations. Their predicted boxes are evaluated before using that step's selected detection for an update. The current-frame states immediately after the update or hold action are also evaluated. For branch $b\in\{W,H\}$, the motion loss is
\begin{equation}
\ell_m^b(\tau)=
1-\operatorname{IoU}(\hat{\bm{b}}_\tau^b,\bm{b}_\tau^{\mathrm{gt}})
+\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\ell_{\mathrm{L1,norm}}
(\hat{\bm{b}}_\tau^b,\bm{b}_\tau^{\mathrm{gt}}).
\label{eq:motion_loss}
\end{equation}
For corner coordinates $(x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2)$, the normalized term is
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\mathrm{L1,norm}}
=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j\in\{1,2\}}
\left(
\frac{|\hat{x}_j^b-x_j^{\mathrm{gt}}|}{\max(\hat{w}^b,\epsilon)}
+\frac{|\hat{y}_j^b-y_j^{\mathrm{gt}}|}{\max(\hat{h}^b,\epsilon)}
\right),
\label{eq:motion_normalization}
\end{equation}
where the frame index is omitted, $\hat{w}^b$ and $\hat{h}^b$ are the width and height of that branch's predicted box, and $\epsilon=10^{-6}$. Each branch therefore uses its own predicted box dimensions for normalization. Summing over the current and future frames with valid ground truth gives $L_m^W$ and $L_m^H$.

\paragraph{Appearance supervision.}
We construct a fixed offline identity prototype $\bm{p}_i$ from the training sequence. Candidate features must satisfy the detection-confidence and ground-truth-overlap thresholds $\theta_{\mathrm{proto}}^{\mathrm{score}}$ and $\theta_{\mathrm{proto}}^{\mathrm{IoU}}$, with at least $N_{\mathrm{proto}}$ candidates available. Features are individually normalized and averaged to obtain an initial normalized mean. We retain the $\lceil0.7N\rceil$ features most similar to that mean, then average and normalize them again to obtain $\bm{p}_i$. This is the standard prototype construction: a current detection that meets the selection criteria can participate in the prototype.

The update branch starts with the normalized full EMA candidate, while the hold branch retains the existing appearance state. Subsequent steps use the same selected detection features and the EMA rule. We evaluate the current state and the states after later updates using
\begin{equation}
\ell_a^b(\tau)=1-\cos(\bm{f}_\tau^b,\bm{p}_i).
\label{eq:appearance_loss}
\end{equation}
Losses are summed over frames with valid target ground truth to obtain $L_a^W$ and $L_a^H$. When a future detection is unavailable, the branch retains its appearance state. Events without a valid identity prototype have no appearance supervision.

\paragraph{Soft update targets.}
For each state type, define the benefit of updating as
\begin{equation}
B_r=L_r^H-L_r^W.
\label{eq:benefit}
\end{equation}
Positive values favor updating, while negative values indicate that retaining the state produces a lower rollout loss. We map this difference to a soft target:
\begin{equation}
y_r^{\mathrm{oracle}}=\sigma(B_r/\tau_r),\qquad
\tau_r=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\operatorname{median}_{B_r\ne0}|B_r|,\tau_{\min},\tau_{\max}
\right).
\label{eq:oracle_soft}
\end{equation}
The temperature is estimated separately for motion and appearance from training benefits. If no nonzero benefits are available, it is set to $\tau_{\max}$.

A small loss difference or incomplete future evidence provides a weak basis for suppressing an update. We therefore weight the target by
\begin{equation}
c_r=\rho_r\min\!\left(\frac{|B_r|}{\kappa\tau_r},1\right),
\label{eq:label_confidence}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_m=n_m/(K+1)$ measures ground-truth coverage over the current and future frames, and $\rho_a=n_a/K$ measures selected-detection coverage over future frames. The final supervision target is
\begin{equation}
y_r=c_r y_r^{\mathrm{oracle}}+(1-c_r).
\label{eq:safe_target}
\end{equation}
This fallback approaches the full update when the comparison is uncertain. Strong suppression therefore requires both a negative update benefit and sufficient supporting evidence. The two targets form $\bm{y}=[y_m,y_a]$; invalid channels are masked during training.

\subsection{Harm-Aware Update Controller}
HUC learns to predict update strength from the information available when a detection is matched. Fig.~\ref{fig:cue} shows how offline supervision is connected to its online decision. Each event is represented by a structured state--association token and an appearance-interaction token, allowing the controller to consider both the track's condition and its agreement with the current detection.

\begin{figure}[pos=!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig3_cue_v12_offset.png}
    \caption{Supervision and prediction for the Harm-Aware Update Controller (HUC). (a) Offline update and hold rollouts provide separate motion and appearance targets based on their later state errors and evidence coverage. (b) HUC encodes recent state--association and appearance-interaction evidence to predict two initial update weights. Auxiliary confidence and risk predictions provide reliability information for RUC.}
    \label{fig:cue}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Event and history encoding.}
The structured input $\bm{s}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{63}$ summarizes association scores and ambiguity, box agreement, motion state and uncertainty, and track history and status. These features describe whether the detection is consistent with the existing track and whether the match is ambiguous. Features are standardized using training-set statistics and mapped through LayerNorm and an MLP to a 64-dimensional token $\bm{v}_t^s$. For unmatched events, detection-dependent fields are set to zero before normalization and a detection-presence indicator identifies the missing input.

For appearance, a shared projection and LayerNorm map the stored feature and detection feature to $\bm{a}_t^{-},\bm{a}_t^d\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$. An MLP encodes their interaction:
\begin{equation}
\bm{v}_t^a=\operatorname{MLP}_a\!\left(
[\bm{a}_t^{-},\bm{a}_t^d,
|\bm{a}_t^{-}-\bm{a}_t^d|,
\bm{a}_t^{-}\odot\bm{a}_t^d]
\right).
\label{eq:appearance_token}
\end{equation}
The two tokens are fused into an event embedding $\bm{e}_t$. HUC adds learned position embeddings to the recent event window and uses a Transformer encoder \cite{vaswani2017attention} to obtain the current representation $\bm{h}_t$. Padding masks exclude missing positions, and reset masks prevent the window from crossing a track reset. Every window ends at the current event, so the representation uses no future frames.

\paragraph{Initial update weights.}
HUC predicts a probability distribution $\bm{\pi}_t$ over five update prototypes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}=\{(1,1),(1,0),(0,1),(0,0),(0.5,0.5)\}.
\label{eq:policy_set}
\end{equation}
The first coordinate controls motion and the second controls appearance. These prototypes represent full updates, updating only one state, retaining both states, and partial updates. A second head predicts a continuous offset $\bm{o}_t$, giving
\begin{equation}
\gbase=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(
\sum_{k=1}^{5}\pi_{t,k}\bm{p}_k+
\delta_{\mathrm{HUC}}\tanh(\bm{o}_t)
\right).
\label{eq:cue_gate}
\end{equation}
The prototype mixture supplies a structured description of the decision, while the offset adjusts its strength. The output remains continuous. To supervise the policy probabilities, the target $\bm{y}$ is converted to
\begin{equation}
\pi_k^*=
\frac{\exp(-\|\bm{p}_k-\bm{y}\|_2^2/\tau_{\mathrm{policy}})}
{\sum_j\exp(-\|\bm{p}_j-\bm{y}\|_2^2/\tau_{\mathrm{policy}})}.
\label{eq:policy_target}
\end{equation}

Two auxiliary heads predict confidence $\bm{c}_t$ and risk $\bm{r}_t^{\mathrm{risk}}$ for use by RUC. Their respective training targets are $[c_m,c_a,\max(c_m,c_a)]$ and $[1-y_m^{\mathrm{oracle}},1-y_a^{\mathrm{oracle}},1-\min(c_m,c_a),|y_m^{\mathrm{oracle}}-y_a^{\mathrm{oracle}}|]$. The event representation is detached before these heads, so auxiliary losses train the heads without changing the representation used for the initial decision.

\subsection{Reliability-Guided Update Calibrator}
RUC refines the initial decision using the separate evidence histories and the estimated reliability of that decision. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:cec}, it summarizes the structured state--association tokens and appearance-interaction tokens independently, then relates the summaries through a reliability token. This preserves access to differences between the two histories that may be less explicit in HUC's fused representation.

\begin{figure}[pos=!ht]
    \centering

Two output heads produce bounded corrections to the motion and appearance weights. Stop-gradient connections isolate HUC from the calibration losses.}
    \label{fig:cec}

Each history is projected to a common feature dimension and augmented with position and evidence-type embeddings. Separate temporal-attention modules use the current token to query the corresponding history, producing summaries $\bm{h}_t^s$ and $\bm{h}_t^a$. The reliability input combines the initial weights, policy probabilities, auxiliary predictions, and current structured features:
\begin{equation}
\bm{u}_t=
[\gbase,\bm{\pi}_t,\bm{c}_t,\bm{r}_t^{\mathrm{risk}},\bm{s}_t]
\in\mathbb{R}^{77}.
\label{eq:reliability_vector}
\end{equation}
A learned projection maps $\bm{u}_t$ to a reliability token $\bm{h}_t^u$. The three tokens $[\bm{h}_t^s,\bm{h}_t^a,\bm{h}_t^u]$ pass through a Transformer layer with evidence-type embeddings. Separate heads read the resulting state--association and appearance representations:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\bm{g}_t=\delta_{\mathrm{RUC}}\tanh\!\left(
[\phi_m(\tilde{\bm{h}}_t^s),\phi_a(\tilde{\bm{h}}_t^a)]
\right).
\label{eq:cec_delta}
\end{equation}
The final weights are
\begin{equation}
\gfinal=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(
\operatorname{stopgrad}(\gbase)+\Delta\bm{g}_t
\right).
\label{eq:final_gate}
\end{equation}

All HUC-produced histories and predictions are detached at the RUC boundary. The final correction layers are initialized to zero, so RUC starts with no change to HUC's output. The bound $\delta_{\mathrm{RUC}}$ limits the change to each weight, while separate output heads allow different corrections for motion and appearance. This design assigns the primary update decision to HUC and trains RUC to make a local refinement.

\subsection{Training and Online Inference}
\paragraph{Training objective.}
Offline preprocessing caches event windows and their rollout targets. For a weight prediction $\hat{\bm{g}}$ and target $\bm{y}$, the update loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_g(\hat{\bm{g}},\bm{y})=
\lambda_{\mathrm{BCE}}\operatorname{BCE}(\hat{\bm{g}},\bm{y})
+\lambda_{\mathrm{MSE}}\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{\bm{g}},\bm{y}).
\label{eq:gate_loss}
\end{equation}
Both terms are averaged over valid channels with the sample weights. HUC receives this loss on $\gbase$, a policy loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{policy}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bm{\pi}^*\|\bm{\pi}_t)$, and auxiliary confidence and risk losses. The auxiliary losses use binary cross-entropy with their corresponding validity masks; policy supervision requires both state targets to be valid.

RUC receives the update loss on $\gfinal$ and a smooth-$\ell_1$ loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adj}}$ toward
\begin{equation}
\Delta\bm{g}_t^*=
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\bm{y}-\operatorname{stopgrad}(\gbase),
-\delta_{\mathrm{RUC}},\delta_{\mathrm{RUC}}
\right).
\label{eq:adjustment_target}
\end{equation}
An additional penalty $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sparse}}$ averages the absolute correction magnitudes to limit unnecessary changes. The full objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}={}&
\lambda_{\mathrm{base}}\mathcal{L}_g^{\mathrm{base}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{policy}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{policy}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{cue}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cue}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{risk}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{risk}}\\
&+\lambda_{\mathrm{final}}\mathcal{L}_g^{\mathrm{final}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adj}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{sparse}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sparse}}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cue}}$ means the auxiliary confidence loss. The two components are trained together, but RUC's losses do not change HUC's parameters. Network dimensions, loss weights, and optimization settings are reported in the implementation details.

\paragraph{Online inference.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:online} summarizes a tracking step. After prediction and association, matched tracks with enough history are processed as a batch. Their current events are formed from the states before updating, combined with the recent histories, and passed through HUC and RUC. The resulting weights control the state updates, after which the events are retained for later frames. Tracks without sufficient matched history follow the full update. In the algorithm, $\mathcal{Z}_t^i$ collects the evidence tokens, auxiliary predictions, and current structured features passed to RUC.

For unmatched tracks, the detection-dependent input is absent and the update weights are set to zero. The unmatched event remains in the history, while prediction and track status updates continue. Event buffers are cleared or masked at resets and track termination. Future rollouts, ground-truth annotations, and offline identity prototypes are used only to construct training supervision and are not required by the online tracker.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Datasets and Metrics}
We evaluate DUETTrack on MOT17 \cite{dendorfer2021motchallenge}, MOT20 \cite{dendorfer2020mot20}, and SportsMOT \cite{cui2023sportsmot}. MOT17 contains pedestrian sequences with varied viewpoints, target scales, and occlusion patterns. MOT20 emphasizes dense crowds and prolonged overlap. SportsMOT contains team-sport footage with abrupt motion, rapid direction changes, moving cameras, and similar uniforms. Together, the three test sets cover general pedestrian tracking, severe crowding, and fast non-linear motion.

HOTA \cite{luiten2021hota} is the primary metric because it balances detection and association quality. We additionally report Detection Accuracy (DetA), Association Accuracy (AssA), IDF1, MOTA \cite{bernardin2008evaluating}, identity switches (IDSW), and fragmentations (Frag). Higher is better for HOTA, DetA, AssA, IDF1, and MOTA, whereas lower is better for IDSW and Frag.

We separate benchmark reporting from method selection. The three public test sets are used only for the final comparison, and all component, supervision, context, and sensitivity experiments are assigned to a held-out validation protocol. This separation is particularly important for counterfactual labels: ground truth and target-aligned future observations may be used to construct training targets on training data, but no test annotation, future test frame, or test result is used as an online input or a training target.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
DUETTrack is integrated only into TrackTrack \cite{shim2025focusing}; we do not claim cross-tracker plug-and-play generalization. The detector, ReID model, data split, candidate construction, association thresholds, post-processing, and track management are identical to the TrackTrack configuration used in the MTUTrack experimental protocol. Specifically, MOT17 and MOT20 use the public YOLOX \cite{ge2021yolox} detector weights provided by ByteTrack \cite{zhang2022bytetrack}, while SportsMOT uses the detector configuration provided by MixSort-OC \cite{cui2023sportsmot}. Test results are obtained from post-processed submissions.

HUC uses a six-event context, 128-dimensional event embeddings, a two-layer Transformer with four heads and feed-forward dimension 256, and $\delta_{\mathrm{HUC}}=0.15$. RUC projects both evidence streams to 128 dimensions, uses four-head temporal and cross-evidence attention, and sets $\delta_{\mathrm{RUC}}=0.05$. The inherited appearance update uses $\alpha=0.95$.

For counterfactual label construction, we set $K=5$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}=0.25$, $\theta_{\mathrm{match}}=0.3$, $N_{\mathrm{proto}}=3$, $\theta_{\mathrm{proto}}^{\mathrm{IoU}}=0.7$, and $\theta_{\mathrm{proto}}^{\mathrm{score}}=0.6$. The benefit temperature in Eq.~\eqref{eq:oracle_soft} is computed independently for motion and appearance with $\tau_{\min}=10^{-3}$ and $\tau_{\max}=0.1$; the confidence scale is $\kappa=2$, and the policy-target temperature is $\tau_{\mathrm{policy}}=0.1$.

For Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate_loss}, $\lambda_{\mathrm{BCE}}=1.0$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{MSE}}=0.1$. In Eq.~\eqref{eq:total_loss}, the selected weights are $\lambda_{\mathrm{base}}=1.0$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{policy}}=0.1$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{cue}}=0.2$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{risk}}=0.1$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{final}}=1.0$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.5$, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{sparse}}=0.01$.

Training uses PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch} and AdamW \cite{kingma2014adam,loshchilov2017decoupled}, with batch size 1024, learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, linear warm-up, gradient clipping at 1.0, and seed 42. MOT17 fine-tuning uses learning rate $10^{-5}$ when initialized from an MOT20 model. All ablations use MOT17-02-FRCNN, MOT17-09-FRCNN, and MOT17-10-FRCNN for the held-out validation protocol.

Offline label generation caches event windows, both rollout losses, coverage values, safe targets, and policy targets. Training therefore does not rerun the tracker inside every optimization step. Inputs are normalized using training statistics, padded positions are masked, and reset masks prevent samples from mixing two trajectory identities. At inference, all matched mature tracks in a frame are processed as one batch. Unmatched events are recorded with zero detection-side fields, while newly initialized tracks retain the original TrackTrack state rule until sufficient history is available.

Under the same end-to-end evaluation setup, TrackTrack runs at 120.60 FPS and DUETTrack at 33.04 FPS. The proposed method remains above real-time video rate, although the temporal calibration introduces a clear computational cost.

On MOT17, DUETTrack improves HOTA from 67.1 to 67.8 and AssA from 68.2 to 69.4. IDF1 rises to 83.6, while IDSW decreases from 822 to 771. Frag changes only slightly, from 1344 to 1338, indicating that the primary MOT17 benefit is improved identity consistency rather than a large change in trajectory recovery.

The association gain is larger on MOT20. DUETTrack obtains 66.4 HOTA, 68.8 AssA, and 82.3 IDF1, improving the corresponding TrackTrack values by 0.7, 1.5, and 1.4 points. It also reduces IDSW by 67 and Frag by 88. Crowding creates assignments in which the best available detection can be shifted or visually mixed; the result is consistent with suppressing harmful state writes after those assignments.

On SportsMOT, DUETTrack reaches 76.5 HOTA and 66.0 AssA. Relative to TrackTrack, IDSW decreases from 2091 to 1867 and Frag decreases from 3995 to 2438, a reduction of approximately 39\% in fragmentation. Sports footage combines abrupt motion, repeated crossings, and nearly identical uniforms, so independently regulating the two state streams is especially relevant.

Across all datasets, DetA and MOTA remain nearly unchanged, whereas AssA, IDF1, and IDSW improve consistently. Because the detector and association procedure are unchanged, this pattern supports the intended scope of DUETTrack: the gain comes primarily from preserving state quality and identity continuity after accepted matches, rather than from detecting more objects.

The small negative differences in MOTA on MOT17 and MOT20 should not be hidden: using the displayed one-decimal values, they are approximately $-0.1$ and $-0.3$ points. MOTA is dominated by false positives and false negatives and can move differently from association-focused metrics. Here, near-constant DetA together with higher HOTA, AssA, and IDF1 and fewer identity switches indicates a tradeoff concentrated in trajectory identity rather than an across-the-board improvement in every metric. On SportsMOT, both MOTA and the association metrics improve.

The three benchmarks also expose different benefits. MOT17 shows a modest but consistent identity improvement with almost unchanged fragmentation, suggesting that many corrected cases are short local ambiguities. MOT20 has larger AssA and IDF1 gains and fewer fragments under dense overlap, where mixed boxes and small assignment margins occur frequently. SportsMOT produces the largest HOTA gain and a $1557$-fragment reduction; this is the setting in which abrupt dynamics and visually similar uniforms most strongly decouple motion and appearance reliability. These interpretations are dataset-level associations, not frame-level causal proof. Figs.~\ref{fig:qual_scenario}--\ref{fig:qual_strips} therefore provide complementary inspections of output continuity in one representative close-contact sequence.

The paired baseline is more informative than rankings against methods with different detectors, appearance encoders, or post-processing. DUETTrack is not best on every individual metric: MOTE reports higher MOT20 MOTA, while DiffMOT reports higher SportsMOT DetA and MOTA. The evidence for the proposed mechanism instead comes from the controlled TrackTrack comparison, where the observation pipeline is fixed and association-oriented measures improve on all three datasets. We therefore claim a consistent improvement to the selected host configuration rather than universal state-of-the-art performance.

Extending the window to ten events degrades all five metrics relative to six events, suggesting that overly long histories dilute the local evidence needed for the current assimilation decision.

\subsection{Robustness to Localization Noise}
We evaluate robustness to corrupted detection geometry on the MOT20 validation sequences used by the inherited perturbation protocol. Position noise displaces the detection center while keeping width and height fixed, whereas size noise perturbs width and height while preserving the center. The perturbation level ranges from 0 to 10 pixels, and resulting boxes are clipped to valid image regions. Because the compared trackers have slightly different clean performance, each curve is normalized by its own 0-pixel HOTA. The resulting HOTA-retention percentage measures degradation from the method's clean operating point rather than conflating robustness with its initial score.

\begin{figure}[pos=!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig8_duettrack_noise_resistance.pdf}
    \caption{Robustness to increasing position noise (left) and size noise (right) on MOT20 validation. Each curve reports the percentage of HOTA retained relative to that tracker's clean 0-pixel result. Higher is better.}
    \label{fig:noise_robustness}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:noise_robustness} shows that DUETTrack retains the highest fraction of clean HOTA at every nonzero perturbation level. At 10-pixel position noise, DUETTrack retains 65.6\% of its clean HOTA, compared with 55.2\% for TrackTrack, 44.6\% for DiffMOT, and 12.4\% for TrackSSM. Under 10-pixel size noise, the corresponding values are 88.2\%, 83.5\%, 83.0\%, and 64.1\%. The gap over the paired TrackTrack baseline therefore reaches 10.3 retention points for position noise and 4.7 points for size noise at the strongest tested perturbation.

Position perturbations cause a steeper decline than size perturbations for all four trackers, consistent with center displacement directly corrupting apparent motion and the state propagated to the next frame. The controlled TrackTrack comparison is the most direct evidence for DUETTrack: both methods share the surrounding detector, association, and track-management pipeline, while the dual-state assimilation model produces a slower degradation curve. DiffMOT and TrackSSM provide additional cross-method context rather than a component-level causal comparison.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented DUETTrack, a post-association framework for utility-guided Kalman-state and appearance-prototype assimilation in online multi-object tracking. Instead of treating every accepted detection as an unconditional write, DUETTrack estimates separate coefficients for the Kalman state and the ReID prototype. CUE learns these coefficients from paired write-versus-hold rollout supervision and a causal history of association, ReID interaction, geometry, Kalman state, and track context. CEC then uses separated state--association and appearance histories with explicit reliability evidence to make bounded coefficient adjustments. Integrated into TrackTrack without changing detection, assignment, or track management, DUETTrack improves HOTA, AssA, and IDF1 on MOT17, MOT20, and SportsMOT while reducing identity switches on all three test sets. The synthetic MOT20 perturbation study further shows that DUETTrack retains a larger fraction of clean HOTA than TrackTrack, DiffMOT, and TrackSSM under both position and size noise. The results indicate that controlling what enters a trajectory after association is a useful complement to improving the association rule itself. Future work will examine occlusion and appearance corruption and relate coefficient trajectories to individual failure modes.
